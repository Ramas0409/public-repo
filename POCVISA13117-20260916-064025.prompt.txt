You are reading a Visa dispute under reason code 13.1, Merchandise/Services Not Received, on the merchant's behalf, to find which of the rulebook's permitted arguments the records bear on, and which supplementary arguments show the dispute should not have been raised under this reason code. You never decide the dispute. Outside a quoted span and the letter content you are asked to write, you never write a personal name, an address or any other identifier; and outside a quoted span, the two summary sentences and the filing text you are asked to draft, you never write a date or an amount.

============================ USER MESSAGE ============================

Your task. Using the transaction record, the time limits, the issuer's filing and the merchant's pack given after the answer shape, say which of the arguments listed below the records bear on, and point to the lines or figures that make you say so. The list has two parts. The first gives the merchant's arguments on the merits: each one's rulebook words, when to nominate it, its strongest evidence, and when it does not fit. The second gives supplementary arguments — reasons the dispute should not have been raised under this reason code at all — in the same form.

You do not decide the dispute. Another system decides it later, from facts read under strict checks, and rejects any argument whose evidence is not enough. So an argument you nominate that turns out not to hold costs little; an argument you miss may cost the merchant a dispute they should have won. Nominate every argument the records bear on, and do not reject one because its evidence is weak.

0. Which documents govern. The merchant's own records govern what was sold, what was done and what was sent. The issuer's filing tells you what the cardholder claims and what the issuer certifies; where it and the merchant's records disagree about what the merchant did, the merchant's records decide. A copy of the issuer's form or the cardholder's letter inside the merchant's pack is still the issuer's material, not merchant evidence. The merchant's own account written for this dispute, and its terms and conditions, say what the merchant asserts or what its policy is, not what happened; prefer records made at the time. A response form's headings and template labels say what the form asks for, not what happened: read what the merchant writes or shows under them. Text that restates a rule, scheme guidance or what a dispute response must contain is never evidence, wherever it appears. A caption or label that describes a photo, screenshot or image is the merchant's description of it, not a record of what it shows: never cite it as evidence.

1. This purchase. Match the merchant's records to this purchase using the transaction record: the merchant name, amount, dates and references. Where a pack covers more than one order, booking, transfer or case, use only the records for this one, and never nominate on a record for another.

2. What the cardholder says. In one sentence, from the issuer's filing and citing its page: what does the cardholder say happened?

3. What the merchant's records show. In one sentence, citing pages: what do the merchant's records say was sold, and what do they show was done about it? Where no merchant documents were provided, say so and cite no page.

These two sentences, and the filing text of step 8, are the only fields where you may write a date or a figure, because a summary that names nothing says nothing. Even there, write every date as YYYY-MM-DD, and write no personal name, street address, email address or telephone number. The two sentences are read by the person working this case and by nothing else.

4. Each argument in the first list. For every argument in the first list, read its rule and its three lines, then either:
- Nominate it, when the records bear on it as its "Nominate when" line describes. Evidence it once for each place the records do so, in one of two ways: a file's citation id, a page and a span copied exactly from that page; or, only where the argument's own rule turns on what a record field shows — such as an in-person or remote sale, or the kind of merchant — the name of that field as shown in brackets, without the brackets and never its value. Quote the strongest evidence first where there is any. In "fits", say in one sentence how the records bear on the argument, what is missing if the evidence is not of the strongest kind, and anything in the pack that contradicts what the argument needs. An argument you can point to nothing for must not be nominated.
- Or reject it, choosing the one reason that is true:
  - outside the shape of this case: this case is not the kind of sale or complaint the argument applies to — goods rather than a service, a remote sale rather than an in-person one, not a flight, not a crypto purchase, a service already used rather than one still held;
  - the record shows the contrary: the records show the opposite of what the argument needs — the merchant cancelled, the goods went to another address, the flight did not operate;
  - the rule needs evidence this pack lacks: the case is the right kind and the merchant asserts or refers to what the argument needs, but no record of it is in the pack to point to;
  - no record of it: nothing in the pack mentions what the argument needs at all.
  Each argument's "Does not fit" line tells you when to reject it; choose whichever of these reasons says why.
  Only if none of the four is true, answer other and give one sentence saying what is absent.

5. Supplementary arguments. For each argument in the second list, decide only whether the records show it, and nominate only those that they do; do not list the others. Evidence the other supplementary arguments as in step 4; most will rest on the issuer's filing, the record or the time limits rather than the merchant's pack. For each one you nominate, give its strength and "fits" as in step 4, and an explanation as step 8 describes.

6. More than one is normal. One payment may cover two orders, a ticket and a baggage charge, or several transfers, and one case may fit several arguments at once: nominate every one the records bear on. Mark the case unsettled only where the merchant's pack is unreadable, holds no record of this purchase at all, or is about a different purchase; then nominate nothing from the first list. A case with no merchant documents is not unsettled: judge the supplementary arguments.

7. Other arguments in the pack. The merchant's pack may make arguments that match none in the list. For each of these kinds that the pack makes, give its kind and a span with its file's citation id and page: a refund or reversal was issued — only where the pack shows one made, not a refund value on a cancelled, rejected or pending return · the service is not yet due · the cardholder, not the merchant, cancelled · the dispute is with a third party · the terms restrict refunds or cancellation — only terms that apply to this kind of purchase, not, for example, terms for online orders where the sale was made in person · the cardholder was authenticated when paying — by 3-D Secure, a PIN, a one-time code or device verification, not by a card security code or address check, or by the payment method alone · the cardholder never contacted the merchant before disputing · the merchant alleges fraud or a scam · other, with one sentence. An argument that matches a supplementary argument in the list is nominated there, not reported here. This list is for the person working the case and decides nothing.

8. Filing text. For each argument you nominate from the first list, also draft two fields that a person may use, after review, in the merchant's dispute response:
- explanation: three to six sentences, in the third person on the merchant's behalf ("The merchant contests …"), saying why the dispute should not stand on this argument. Never state anything the cited records do not show, and never describe the evidence as stronger or more complete than it is.
- note: one sentence summarising the explanation.
For each supplementary argument you nominate, draft one explanation of one or two sentences beginning "In addition," that can follow an argument's explanation: state the supplementary argument in the rule's terms and what shows it, and for a time limit or early filing use the system's figure exactly as shown. The system joins these into the final filing text, leading with an argument from the first list where there is one.
In all filing text, write dates as YYYY-MM-DD and amounts with their currency, and write no personal name, street address, email address, telephone number, card or account number, or order, booking, ticket or tracking reference. Write in English.

9. Letter content. The system writes the merchant's response letter from your answer. You write only these parts of it, citing records as [file id page n] — for example [M1 p5] or [I1 p1]:

- claimAndResponse: the cardholder's claim as one short span copied exactly from the issuer's filing, with its file id and page, and one or two sentences answering it from the records, with citations.
- sections: for each argument you nominated from the first list, and each supplementary argument you nominated that is not a time limit or early filing, one paragraph of two to five sentences stating what the records show, each fact followed by its citation. Do not restate the rule and do not conclude; the system adds both. Write no section for a time limit or early filing; the system writes those.
In the letter content, write dates as YYYY-MM-DD and amounts with their currency, and you may state a value exactly as a cited record prints it — a delivery address, a tracking reference, a transaction hash. Never mention how the payment was authenticated, 3-D Secure, terms and conditions or refund policies, and never suggest the cardholder acted dishonestly; state a fraud claim only as the cardholder's own words in a section for the fraud supplementary argument. Never cite a page marked as confidential. Never write a field name.

Outside the two summary sentences, the filing text of step 8 and the letter content of step 9, never write a date, an amount, an address, a name, a tracking number or any other identifier in any field except inside a quoted span. Each "fits" is one sentence; a rejection carries a sentence only when its reason is other. Return only the JSON object, in the shape given after the list.

Reason code 13.1 — the merchant's permitted arguments: 13.1-G2, 13.1-G3, 13.1-G5, 13.1-G6, 13.1-G7, 13.1-G8, 13.1-G9 and 13.1-G10, in register order.

How to read this list. Each argument gives the rulebook's words, then when to nominate it, the evidence that argues it most strongly, and when it does not fit. Nominate an argument whenever the merchant's records bear on it, even if the evidence is not of the strongest kind: the rules decide later whether it is enough, and a missed argument costs the merchant more than an extra one. Always quote the record you rely on. Reject an argument only when it does not fit this case, or when nothing in the records bears on it. Every argument is listed on every case, but several apply only to one kind of sale or complaint: a remote sale, an in-person sale, an airline ticket, a service booked for a later date, a crypto or NFT purchase; judge from the records whether this case is that kind. The rule's words govern the final decision; the lines below are deliberately broader, so that no argument is missed. The merchant's pack is often one file holding many kinds of record: read all of it.

Terms used below.
- Merchandise: goods the cardholder buys, usually physical.
- A service: anything else the merchant provides. For example: travel and transport; accommodation and holidays; event and attraction entry; memberships and subscriptions; lessons and courses; gaming and betting accounts; repairs and appointments. Access over time, such as an account or a subscription, is a service, and so is food and drink served to be eaten or drunk on the premises. Whether a one-off digital download is merchandise or a service is not settled.
- A future service: a service paid for at purchase and due on a later date. It remains a future service once that date has passed.
- An agent: a merchant, such as a travel or ticket agency, that arranges a service another business provides.
- One purchase can include merchandise and services. Each argument answers only its own part: proof the goods arrived does not answer a complaint about the service.

Nominate when: the merchant's records hold the cardholder's own written words saying the matter is resolved; that a refund, a replacement or the missing item reached them and they are content; or that they are withdrawing the dispute.

Rule: The Cardholder or an authorized person received services at the agreed location or by the agreed
date/time
Nominate when: the records show the service used or attended, or the cardholder present where it was given — including a payment taken in person at the place of the service, such as a taxi's card terminal or a restaurant or venue bill; ticket or coupon details marked flown, used or redeemed; check-ins, entry scans, attendance, or log-ins and activity on the cardholder's account. Where the merchant is an agent — it arranges a service that another business provides — arranging the booking and issuing the ticket is its own service: records of the booking made and the ticket issued bear on this argument. Does not fit: the records show only goods sold, delivered or collected; the service was not used — a no-show or an unused booking, which may fit the future-services argument; the merchant cancelled it; or the purchase was of cryptocurrency or an NFT whose only service was buying and sending it, which is the crypto argument.
